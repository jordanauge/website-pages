Building a custom beech bookshelf for a tight corridor

2026-06-16 2026-11-02 Woodworking #woodworking #beech #bookshelf #rubio-monocoat #freecad
Summary

A custom floor-level beech shelf built from rough-sawn stock to fit an awkward corridor space, finished with Rubio Monocoat Natural.

Introduction

This article documents a small custom bookshelf built from raw beech to fill an unused strip of corridor wall. The shelf had to align with an existing recessed unit, dodge a door that eats into the passage, and hold children’s books of very different heights.

The project was also a personal excuse to work with rough lumber for the first time: buy a board, dimension it, join it, and finish it without hiding the material behind paint.

Motivation

We had been looking for a practical way to store kids’ books in the corridor. The space was real, but awkward: a door swings into the passage and steals about 16 cm of depth on one side, and an older recessed shelf with a frame sits roughly 70 cm above the floor.

That combination ruled out an off-the-shelf solution. Anything deep enough to be useful would collide with the door swing; anything shallow enough would look like an afterthought. A custom piece made more sense than forcing a catalogue dimension into a place that was never designed for one.

I had also wanted to build something from raw wood for a long time. This was a good first project: one board, straight joinery, visible structure, and a finish that leaves the grain readable.

The constraint: geometry before aesthetics

Before thinking about proportions, I wrote down the hard limits:

Constraint Value Consequence
Door encroachment ~16 cm into corridor depth Shelf depth must stay shallow on the affected side
Existing recessed shelf Frame at ~70 cm above floor Upper bound for overall height
Book sizes Mixed picture books and taller volumes Shelves cannot all share one height
Placement Floor level No plinth; originally considered a 7 cm raise, then dropped it

The shelf therefore had to be shallow, asymmetrical, and split into compartments with different clear heights. Floor placement kept the construction simpler and avoided a visual gap under the unit in an already busy corridor.

Design

The design is deliberately asymmetric. Each bay is sized for the books it is meant to receive rather than for an even rhythm across the front.

I modelled the piece in FreeCAD. The plan set is still evolving, but the intent is to publish:

  • a top view with overall dimensions and bay widths,
  • a front elevation with shelf heights,
  • a short parts list derived from the cut list.

Processing the wood

The starting material was a single rough-sawn beech board, 3 m long and 36 mm thick. The first job was to bring it down to a usable thickness for interior shelving.

Resawing

The target was roughly 12 mm finished stock. My smaller circular saw can only reach about 75 mm of cutting depth, so resawing a 36 mm board in one pass was not realistic. I used the larger saw instead and split the board in half.

Surfacing

After resawing, the faces were still far from furniture-ready:

  1. Thickness planing (raboteuse) to bring both faces parallel and close to final thickness.
  2. Sanding (ponceuse) to remove milling marks before dimensioning parts.

Only then did it make sense to cut the uprights, shelves, and back parts to length. Doing the rough work on one long board is much easier than trying to plane many short pieces individually.

Assembly

I joined the carcass with biscuits (lamelles) and glue. Biscuits are a good fit here: the loads are modest, alignment matters more than sheer strength, and the joints stay hidden once the shelf is assembled.

Glue is less forgiving. On raw beech, any squeeze-out that reaches the visible face is difficult to remove cleanly. A trace that would disappear under paint stays visible under an oil finish. I clamped the assembly with:

  • a large wooden framing square to keep the carcass square,
  • long straps to pull the joints closed while the glue cured.

Finishing with Rubio Monocoat Natural

For the finish I chose Rubio Monocoat in the Natural tone. The goal was a light, warm, slightly yellowish reading of the beech — not a film on top of the wood.

Choosing the product and quantity

Buying the finish was harder than expected. Rubio publishes a colour chart, but it is still difficult to predict how a given oil will read on a specific species. Beech can shift warmer or greener depending on sanding, glue residue, and how many coats you apply.

Quantity is another guess. I ordered the small bottle and the dedicated application sponge from acheter-rubio.fr.

The order itself was eventually fine, but the delivery experience was poor. I received no parcel collection code because the checkout had no phone number or email tied to the shipment. Chat support was effectively unreachable, and email answers were slow. The vendor did re-send the parcel, so I would still call them serious — just not frictionless.

Application

Rubio is meant to be applied in small batches: work a section, wipe back, move on. That suits a piece with corners and compartments better than trying to coat the whole shelf at once.

The practical upside showed up later. When glue marks appeared after the first coat, I sanded the affected areas and reapplied Rubio without any visible boundary between old and new work. A light pass with 000 steel wool after curing gave the surface I wanted.

In the end I used about three quarters of the small bottle. The only real downside was smell: the corridor carried it for several days.

Conclusion

This was a useful first raw-wood project. The corridor constraints forced a custom shape that would have been annoying to approximate with bought furniture, and the construction stayed simple enough to finish in a reasonable time.

The main lessons:

  • Dimension rough stock before cutting parts. Resawing and planing one long board is far less tedious than chasing thickness on many short pieces.
  • Treat glue like a finish risk. On bare wood, squeeze-out is not a cosmetic detail; it is a defect you will see forever.
  • Rubio rewards patience. Small batches, easy local repairs, good rendering on beech — but plan for odour and buy from a vendor you can actually reach if delivery goes wrong.

I am happy with how the shelf sits in the corridor. Next step is to publish the cleaned-up FreeCAD plans so the dimensions are easy to reuse or adapt.
